For a long time my homelab was one machine trying to do two jobs, and it was quietly bad
at both.

It held things I'd have been genuinely upset to lose: photos, backups, the media library
the rest of the household expects to work. It was also where I tried things. Any new
service, any upgrade, any half-understood idea went onto the same box. Which meant every
experiment carried a small tax of guilt: if I broke this, I break something that
matters, and someone will notice, and it'll be my fault at an inconvenient hour.

That tax is small enough to ignore and large enough to change your behaviour. I did
fewer experiments. I did them more carefully, which sounds like a virtue and isn't. I
mean I did them *timidly*, avoiding the interesting parts. I put off upgrades. I built
up a quiet backlog of things I'd been meaning to try and hadn't, all for the same
unarticulated reason.

## Two jobs, in conflict

The two jobs are genuinely incompatible.

**The first is operating.** Keep it up. Don't lose data. Notice before anyone else does.
Change things slowly and reversibly. This job rewards conservatism, and the whole point
is that it's boring.

**The second is learning.** Break it deliberately. Try the thing you don't understand.
Find out what happens when a node disappears mid-write, because reading about it teaches
you approximately nothing. This job rewards recklessness, and the whole point is that
you find the edges.

Ask one machine to do both and the operating job wins every time, because it has
consequences attached and the learning job doesn't. You end up with a very stable server
and no education.

## What I actually did

Split it in two.

The **Unraid box** holds everything I'd be annoyed to lose and runs the services other
people depend on. Thirty terabytes, several dozen containers, half a dozen VMs. That
machine has one job: work. It gets boring, well-understood changes on a slow cadence. I
am not clever on that box.

The **k3s cluster** is the other half. Six Raspberry Pi 4s, gigabit, nothing precious on
it. That's where new things go, and where I'm free to take a node down at 11pm on a
Tuesday without apologising to anyone.

The rule that makes it work is embarrassingly simple:

> If someone else in the house would notice it was down, it doesn't live on the cluster.

That's it. Not a technical boundary but a *social* one. It turns out to be the only
criterion that actually holds, because it's the one you can evaluate honestly at the
moment you're deciding where to deploy something.

## What changed

The obvious thing is that I experiment more. The less obvious thing is that I experiment
*differently*. When breaking something is free, you stop reaching for the safe version
of the test. You pull the power on a node instead of draining it politely. You let etcd
get unhappy on purpose to see what unhappy looks like. You find out what your monitoring
misses, which is the only way to learn what your monitoring misses.

Almost everything I know about operating Kubernetes under stress, I learned on hardware
where the stakes were zero. That transferred upward completely.

The other change is that the Unraid box got *more* reliable, not less, because it
stopped being the place where I tried things. Half its historical incidents were me.

## The third category

There's a category the split doesn't cover, and it took me a while to notice: things
that have to survive me tinkering with the lab at all.

This site is one of them. It would be neater to serve it from the cluster it talks
about, and there's a pleasing recursion to that, and I was tempted. But then an evening
of experimenting takes my portfolio offline, and the failure mode is that the page
explaining my infrastructure is down *because* of my infrastructure. That's a worse joke
than it is a demonstration.

So it's static, on a CDN, entirely outside both halves. Not everything that *could* run
on your own hardware *should*. Deciding which is which is most of the skill.

## If you're building one

Don't start by picking hardware. Start by writing down which of your two jobs each thing
you own is for, and notice how many of them are currently doing both. That list is the
design.
