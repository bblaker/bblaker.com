Punchline is a time card and invoicing app I built for exactly one user. It works, I use
it, and the obvious next step is an MCP server so that logging time happens in a sentence
instead of a form. The backend is already a Worker with a typed data layer, so exposing it
is a thin surface rather than a second implementation. An afternoon, maybe.

I sketched the tool list in about four minutes:

```
logTime(client, minutes, note)
listUnbilled(client, since)
clientSummary(client)
openInvoices()
createInvoice(client, entryIds)       <-
markPaid(invoiceId, amount, date)     <-
voidInvoice(invoiceId)                <-
```

Then I sat and looked at the last three for a while, because something was wrong with them
and it took me an embarrassing amount of time to say what.

It is not that they are writes. `logTime` is a write. It is not that they are
destructive, because `voidInvoice` is the only one that removes anything and it is the
least alarming of the three. And it is certainly not that they are harder to build. They
are the same amount of code as the rest. That is exactly why I nearly shipped them: by
every measure I was actually applying, they looked identical to the tools above them.

## The test is who finds out

Here is the distinction I landed on, and I think it generalises well past invoicing.

**Log ninety minutes to the wrong client.** That is my problem. I notice it, or I don't
and the monthly total looks slightly off, and either way I fix it in ten seconds. The
error begins and ends inside a database I own. Nobody else ever knows it happened.

**Issue an invoice to the wrong client.** That is *their* problem now. It is in their
inbox. Depending on the client it is also in their AP system, in a shared folder, or
forwarded to an accountant who has never heard of me and now has a question. I cannot
unsend it. I can only apologise for it, and the apology is the artifact they remember.

Those two failures are not different in degree. They are different in kind, and the thing
that separates them has nothing to do with reads, writes, or how much code sits behind the
call. It is this:

> When this goes wrong, who finds out, and do they find out from me?

If the answer is "me, from myself", automate it freely. If the answer is "someone else,
before I know about it", that is a different category of operation and it deserves a
different level of ceremony. Everything downstream follows from that one question.

## Reversibility is the wrong frame

The instinct is to reach for "is it reversible", and it sounds rigorous. It isn't,
because it quietly asks about the database.

I can delete an invoice from D1 in about forty milliseconds. I cannot delete it from
someone's expectations, their inbox, or their recollection that I once billed them twice.
The database is not where the consequence lives. Anything that leaves my systems is
irreversible in the only sense that matters, no matter how clean the rollback is.

That reframing covers more than invoices. Sending email, opening a pull request against a
shared repo, posting to a channel, deploying, filing a ticket that pages someone. All of
them are cheap to undo technically and impossible to undo socially, and it is the second
one that decides whether a thing should happen because a sentence was parsed correctly.

## "Just add a confirmation step"

This is the obvious objection and I want to take it seriously, because I believe it does
less work than it appears to.

A confirmation prompt is useful when the thing it interrupts is unexpected. In an agent
loop it is almost never unexpected. I asked for this. I am in flow. The model has just
told me, fluently and plausibly, what it is about to do and why that is the right call.
The confirm button is the path of least resistance and I will press it, because pressing
it is how I continue doing the thing I asked for.

We already know this about deterministic software. Everyone has clicked through a dialog
they did not read. The version where the dialog comes attached to a confident, articulate
justification is not an improvement on that, and I would rather not find out how many
times I can be convinced before I stop reading.

So the confirmation stays, but it is not load-bearing. The actual control is that the
capability is not there.

## What ships

Reads, and `logTime`. That is the whole first version.

Issuing invoices, marking them paid, and voiding them stay behind the UI, where I have to
open a tab, see the client name next to the amount, and click a button that only does
that one thing. That is maybe fifteen seconds of friction a handful of times a month.

I want to be precise that this friction is not a failure to finish the integration. It is
the integration. Opening the tab *is* the control, and fifteen seconds is the cheapest
insurance I will ever buy against a conversation that begins "hey, quick question about
this invoice."

## The part that costs me

I should be honest that this is not a free decision, because the operations with
consequences are exactly the operations worth automating.

"Invoice everyone for last month" is a twenty minute task I would genuinely love to
collapse into a sentence. It is the single most valuable thing an agent could do in this
app, and it is the one thing I am refusing it. The cheap automation is the stuff that only
touches me. I am aware that I have drawn the line precisely around the valuable part.

I am also aware that "for now" is doing real work in all of this. I expect to move the
line. I will probably start with `createInvoice` producing a draft that I still have to
release by hand, which keeps the generation automated and the sending human, and that is
likely where it settles for a long time.

But I wanted the starting position written down, by date, before I had any experience to
rationalise with. Everyone is publishing what they connected their agent to. Almost nobody
is publishing what they decided not to, and the second list is the one that reflects
whether you thought about it at all.
