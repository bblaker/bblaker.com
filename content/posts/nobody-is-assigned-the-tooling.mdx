Every organisation I've worked in has had a pile of work that would make everyone
measurably faster, that everyone agrees is worth doing, and that never gets done.

The local dev setup that takes a day and a half and a colleague's help. The deploy that
requires three manual steps someone has to remember. The test suite everyone runs with
`-k` and a guess. The script four teams have each written a worse private version of.

Nobody is against fixing any of it. It just never gets assigned, and it's worth being
precise about why, because "people don't care about developer experience" is the wrong
diagnosis and leads to the wrong fix.

## The structural reason

Tooling work has a distribution problem. The cost lands on one team; the benefit spreads
across all of them.

If I'm running a team with a roadmap, spending three weeks on a build system that makes
eight teams 10% faster is, from where I'm measured, three weeks of not shipping. The
return is real and large and it lands almost entirely on other people's numbers. Do that
consistently and I look like the team that doesn't deliver, while being the team that
delivered the most.

So it goes on the list, stays on the list, and gets bumped every quarter by something
with a name attached. Not because anyone is wrong at any step. Each individual
prioritisation call is defensible. The aggregate is that a whole category of high-return
work is structurally unownable.

You can see this most clearly in how it *does* eventually get built: by someone annoyed
enough to do it off the books, usually at the wrong time, usually alone, and often
without the mandate to make anyone actually adopt it.

## Why the obvious fixes underdeliver

**"Make a platform team."** Helps, and it's what I'd do, but it can just relocate the
problem. A platform team with a roadmap handed down from elsewhere ends up building the
things that were legible enough to make someone's plan, which are rarely the same as the
things costing everyone an hour a day. The friction that matters most is often too small
to write a ticket about and too constant to notice.

**"Twenty percent time."** Diffuse. Tooling wants sustained attention from someone who
understands the whole path, not scattered hours from people optimising their own local
annoyance.

**"Let engineers self-serve."** This is how you get the fourth private copy of the same
script.

What actually works, in my experience, is less satisfying: someone with enough seniority
to absorb the roadmap cost decides this is real work, names it, and defends it when it
looks like nothing is shipping. It's a political act more than a technical one.

## Why I keep doing it anyway

Most of what I've built that I'm proud of falls in this category, and it isn't altruism.
It's that this work has properties I like.

The feedback is immediate and honest. Nobody is polite about a tool. If it's bad, people
route around it, silently, and you find out by noticing they've stopped using it. That's
a harsher and better signal than most product work ever gets.

The constraints are real. Tooling has to work on everyone's machine, in CI, on the one
person's setup that's different, and on the worst day of the release cycle. That's a
proper engineering problem, and it's the reason "just write a script" so rarely holds.

And the leverage is genuinely nonlinear in a way very little else is. Ten minutes off
every deploy, times a team, times a year, is a headcount. The arithmetic isn't subtle.
It's just that nobody's compensation is attached to it.

## What I'd tell someone earlier in their career

Build it. Not instead of your actual work. Alongside it, small, and for yourself first.

The version of this that fails is the six-week ambitious platform nobody asked for. The
version that works is a thing that solves your own daily annoyance, that you use every
day, that someone notices over your shoulder and asks for. Adoption you didn't have to
argue for is the only durable kind.

Then be slightly shameless about it. The work is invisible by nature, which is the
entire problem, so if you don't say what it saved, in numbers, nobody will infer it. Not
self-promotion. Making legible something that genuinely happened and otherwise won't be
counted.
